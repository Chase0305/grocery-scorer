# Stevia / steviol glycosides (E 960; Reb A, Reb M, "stevia leaf extract", Truvia, Zevia, Splenda Stevia)

*Researched 2026-10-05 by the scheduled research agent. ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and sciencedirect.com were blocked by the network, so the meta-analyses, the NutriNet-Santé papers, the 2024 microbiome trials and the 2026 narrative review are summarised from search results and abstracts only.*

## Verdict

Purified stevia sweeteners have the cleanest safety record of any high-intensity sweetener: regulators (JECFA, EFSA, FDA) agree on an ADI that real diets rarely approach, two 2024 human trials found no change in gut bacteria, and trials show neutral-to-slightly-favourable effects on blood glucose and blood pressure. The one serious caveat is not stevia-specific: WHO (2023) advises against using *any* non-sugar sweetener, stevia included, for weight control, because sweeteners in general have not helped long-term weight loss and are associated (low certainty, likely partly reverse causation) with diabetes and heart disease in cohorts. Leaving stevia unflagged is defensible.

## Evidence grade

**Weak** evidence of harm at real intakes (mostly animal or in-vitro, or borrowed from the whole non-sugar-sweetener class); **Moderate** evidence that it is metabolically neutral in humans over weeks to months. The WHO class-wide recommendation is itself rated "low certainty" and "conditional".

## What the evidence says

**Regulators**
- JECFA group ADI 0-4 mg/kg bw/day as steviol equivalents (about 12 mg/kg of Reb A, 10 mg/kg of stevioside). Mean intake among users is 0.4-0.7 mg/kg/day and high consumers 1.1-1.7 mg/kg/day - well under the ADI ([IFIC summary of JECFA/intake modelling](https://ific.org/resources/articles/everything-you-need-to-know-about-stevia-sweeteners/); [PMC8600158 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8600158/), via search summary).
- EFSA 2024 refused industry requests to raise the ADI to 6 or 16 mg/kg, keeping 4 mg/kg; it noted conservative exposure estimates could exceed the ADI only for high-consuming toddlers ([EFSA Journal 2024, PMC11533382](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11533382/)). EFSA had already concluded in 2010 that steviol glycosides are not genotoxic or carcinogenic. A 2025 EFSA opinion addressed specifications for enzyme-/fermentation-made glycosides (E 960c) - i.e. the Reb M type used by Splenda Stevia ([EFSA Journal 2025](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.2903/j.efsa.2025.9396)).
- FDA: high-purity (>= 95%) steviol glycosides are GRAS; whole stevia leaf and crude extracts are **not** approved and are subject to Import Alert 45-06, "due to inadequate toxicological information" ([FDA sweeteners page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [Import Alert 45-06](https://open-gov.usebase.io/government/fda-import-alerts/119); [FDA regulatory approach, Food Chem Toxicol 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278691518307063)). So the "stevia leaf extract" on a US label is a refined, purified extract - natural in origin, but not a minimally processed plant.

**Human trials (metabolic)**
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs (Nutrients 2019): steviol glycosides gave a small, non-significant fall in BMI vs placebo, a fall in fasting glucose of about 4 mg/dL, no change in HbA1c, and a reduction in systolic blood pressure; heterogeneity was high and the BP effect came from high-dose stevioside trials in hypertensives ([PMC6770957](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6770957/)). An older DARE-appraised meta-analysis of cardiovascular risk factors reached a similar "small effects, robustness limited" conclusion ([NBK291841](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK291841/)).
- Food & Function 2024 meta-analysis of glucose metabolism and a 2024 meta-analysis on glucose/HbA1c: small fasting-glucose reduction, low certainty ([RSC Food & Function 2024](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2024/fo/d3fo04695h); [Diabetes Metab Syndr 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S187140212400153X)).
- 12-week RCT in Indian adults replacing table sugar with a stevia tabletop sweetener: modest weight/waist and glucose benefits vs sugar ([Nutrients 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/7/1744)). Note the comparison is against sugar, which is the realistic use case.
- 2026 narrative review of human trials of Reb A/Reb M as a sugar-reduction tool: neutral on glycaemia and appetite, small benefits when replacing sugar ([Nutrients 2026, doi 10.3390/nu18122002](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18122002); [PMC13305536](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13305536/)). Industry-adjacent narrative reviews are common in this field; weight these below the independent meta-analyses.

**Gut microbiome**
- Suez et al., *Cell* 2022 (n = 120, 2 weeks, doses below ADI): all four sweeteners shifted stool/oral microbiome; saccharin and sucralose impaired glucose tolerance; **stevia and aspartame did not significantly change glycaemic responses** at group level, affecting only some individuals ([Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(22)00994-1); [expert reaction](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-study-looking-at-the-effect-of-non-nutritive-sweeteners-on-human-microbiomes-and-glycaemic-levels/)).
- Singh et al., *Nutrients* 2024 (12-week open-label RCT, 5 drops twice daily) and a 4-week crossover trial: no change in microbiota composition, diversity or short-chain fatty acids; subtle functional shifts only ([University of Manchester record](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/consumption-of-the-non-nutritive-sweetener-stevia-for-12-weeks-do/); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240122/Sweetener-Stevia-does-not-alter-gut-microbiota-composition-confirms-new-study.aspx)).
- The "stevia harms gut bacteria" claims trace mainly to rodent work, e.g. low-dose Reb A altering microbiota and dopamine reward signalling in rats ([PubMed 31159256](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31159256/)) - *animal only* - and in-vitro reviews ([PMC9028423](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9028423/)).

**Cohorts and the WHO position (class-wide, not stevia-specific)**
- WHO 2023 guideline: "WHO suggests that non-sugar sweeteners not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases" - conditional, low certainty, explicitly including stevia and stevia derivatives; does not apply to people with diabetes ([WHO guideline](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/e567a191-33a4-44ff-8b37-788a4e432764/content); [NBK592246](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK592246/)). The underlying WHO-commissioned review (Rios-Leyvraz & Montez 2022) had only two stevia RCTs (dental caries, insulin) and no stevia-specific cohort data ([WHO IRIS 353064](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/353064)). The cohort harms it relied on came from mixed sweetener/diet-drink exposure. The UK's SACN and others criticised extrapolating that to every sweetener ([SACN statement](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67ea97b3ea9f8afd8105627d/sacn-position-statement-on-non-sugar-sweeteners.pdf); [Advances in Nutrition 2024](https://advances.nutrition.org/article/S2161-8313(24)00162-5/fulltext)).
- NutriNet-Santé (n ~ 103,000, brand-level dietary records) linked aspartame, acesulfame-K and sucralose to cardiovascular disease and cancer, but reported molecule-level results only for those three, not for steviol glycosides (the papers were blocked; this is from their abstracts and search summaries) ([BMJ 2022 CVD, PMC9593923](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9593923/); [PLOS Med 2022 cancer](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1003950)). So there is no large cohort signal against stevia - but also no cohort reassurance; the question is simply unstudied.

**On the other side (in vitro)**
- Fermented stevia extract killed pancreatic cancer cells in culture (Hiroshima 2025) ([IJMS, PMC12071683](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12071683/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/07/250722044704.htm)). *Cell culture only; says nothing about eating stevia sweeteners.* Do not treat as a health benefit.

## Dose in real products

- Stevia is 200-350x sweeter than sugar, so amounts are tiny: a tabletop packet holds tens of milligrams of glycoside (most of the packet is erythritol or dextrose bulk); a stevia soda contains well under 230 mg of extract per can ([search summary for Zevia](https://healthreporter.com/is-zevia-healthy/); brand does not disclose exact mg).
- ADI for a 70 kg adult = 280 mg steviol equivalents = about 840 mg Reb A per day. Even three stevia sodas plus a few packets stays under it; measured high-consumer intakes are under half the ADI. Toddlers are the only group EFSA flags as possibly exceeding it in worst-case modelling.
- Tested doses in the trials (drops, sachets, 100s of mg/day) match real use, which is unusual and reassuring compared with the 30 g erythritol or rodent megadose studies.

## How the scorer treats it today

- **Not flagged.** No category in `scripts/flagged_ingredients.json` matches "stevia", "steviol", "rebaudioside" or "rebiana". SCORING_NOTES.md decision 3 left stevia unflagged as "a philosophy call".
- Not in `_SWEETENER_TERMS`, so `SWEETENER_PRIMARY_CAP` doesn't apply - but no catalog product leads with stevia except one Zevia whose label lists ingredients by percentage oddly; tabletop "stevia" products lead with erythritol or dextrose.
- Effect: a stevia-only drink scores like an unsweetened one on ingredients, e.g. Zevia sodas 67-75; diet drinks with sucralose/ace-K lose 24 points of flagged sub-score instead. The Splenda Stevia packets already sit at 40 via the dextrose-first sweetener cap; Truvia/Wholesome Pantry blends score 75-96 because of the unflagged **erythritol**, not the stevia (that is handled by the erythritol recommendation of 2026-10-04).

## Recommendation

**Keep as is (do not flag stevia).** Severity 2 (the artificial-sweetener level) is clearly not supported: the specific concerns behind sucralose's flag (impaired glucose tolerance in Suez 2022, sucralose-6-acetate genotoxicity, NutriNet-Santé CHD signal) do not exist for stevia, and its human trials are neutral or mildly favourable. A severity-1 "non-sugar sweetener" flag on stevia would only be justified if the owner wants to adopt WHO's class-wide stance (sweeteners as such don't help health, keep the sweet habit) - that is a philosophy choice, not an evidence-driven one; it would cost about 4-5 points on 326 stevia-only products (Zevia 75 -> about 70, Poppi, Olipop-style drinks, zero-sugar yogurts). If the owner ever does that, it should go into a separate low-severity category so the sucralose/aspartame flag stays distinct.

Optional, minor: add "stevia leaf extract" / "steviol glycosides" / "reb m" to whatever banner text explains sweeteners, so shoppers see the product is sweetened even though it is not penalised. Whole-leaf stevia is not sold as a food ingredient in the US, so no separate rule is needed.
