# Monk fruit (luo han guo) extract (Siraitia grosvenorii; mogrosides, mogroside V; "monk fruit extract", "monk fruit juice concentrate", Monk Fruit In The Raw, Lakanto)

*Researched 2026-10-06 by the scheduled research agent. ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, foodstandards.gov.au and researchsquare.com were blocked by the network, so the EFSA 2019 opinion, the FSANZ A1129 safety report, the 2025 systematic review and the 2025 EU-regulation review are summarised from search results and abstracts only.*

## Verdict

There is no human evidence that monk fruit extract harms health at the amounts people eat, and the few small human trials show it behaves like other zero-calorie sweeteners: no rise in blood sugar or insulin compared with sugar. The honest caveat is that its safety file is **thin**, thinner than stevia's: the US (GRAS) and Australia/NZ found no hazard, but the EU's EFSA in 2019 declined to call a purified extract safe because of an unresolved testis finding in one 90-day rat study and no long-term studies. That is a data gap, not a demonstrated harm; leaving monk fruit unflagged, like stevia, is defensible.

## Evidence grade

**Weak** evidence of harm (one equivocal high-dose rat finding; nothing in humans). **Weak-to-Moderate** evidence that it is metabolically neutral in humans (five small, short RCTs). Overall the topic is best described as *under-studied* rather than contested: no good study shows harm, and no long-term study shows safety.

## What the evidence says

**What it is.** Mogrosides are cucurbitane glycosides from a Chinese gourd; mogroside V is the main sweet one, about 250-300x sweeter than sugar. Gut bacteria strip the sugars off within about 24 h to the aglycone **mogrol**, and some mogroside V is absorbed into the blood ([in-vitro metabolism study, PubMed 33387567](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33387567/); [EFSA 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7008860/)). Because it is systemically available, toxicologists treat it more like an additive needing full testing than like a non-absorbed fibre.

**Regulators**
- **FDA (US):** several GRAS notices (extracts from 3.5% up to ~95% mogroside V and the juice concentrate) received "no questions" letters, e.g. GRN 627 and GRN 629 ([FDA GRN 627 letter](https://www.fda.gov/food/gras-notice-inventory/agency-response-letter-gras-notice-no-grn-000627); [GRN 629 dossier](https://www.fda.gov/files/food/published/GRAS-Notice-000629---Siraitia-grosvenorii-Swingle-%28Luo-Han-Guo%29-fruit-extract.pdf); [FDA GRN document](https://fda.gov/media/119443/download)). GRAS notices are written by the manufacturer and FDA does not do its own safety assessment; "no questions" is a lower bar than an EFSA opinion. One dossier estimated the highest 90th-percentile mogroside V intake at about 4.9 mg/kg bw/day (infants 8-12 months, worst case).
- **FSANZ (Australia/NZ, A1129, approved 2018):** "no public health and safety issues"; no adverse effects at the highest doses in subchronic studies; a reproductive/developmental screening study in rats found no effects on fertility, pups, sexual differentiation or thyroid; set an **ADI "not specified"** (the most relaxed category, meaning no identifiable hazard) ([FSANZ approval report](https://mta-sts.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/food-standards-code/applications/Documents/A1129%20Approval%20report.pdf); [FSANZ executive summary](https://mta-sts.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/food-standards-code/applications/Documents/A1129-ExecSummary.pdf)).
- **EFSA (EU, 2019, FAF Panel):** the toxicity database is **"insufficient to conclude on the safety"** of a purified extract as a food additive. Reasons: (1) changes in the testes in a 90-day rat study with a 52% mogroside V extract "cannot be dismissed", and 90 days does not cover a full rat spermatogenesis cycle, so a longer study at higher doses was needed; (2) the genotoxicity tests did not adequately cover mogrol and other gut-bacterial metabolites (the extracts themselves were negative in Ames and chromosome-damage tests); (3) given systemic absorption, chronic/carcinogenicity data "would have been warranted". EFSA's exposure estimates for the proposed EU uses were high (P95 up to 71.5 mg mogroside V/kg/day) because the applicant proposed broad use ([EFSA Journal 2019;17:5921](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7008860/); [Aberdeen record](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/safety-of-use-of-monk-fruit-extract-as-a-food-additive-in-differe/)). Note the asymmetry: FSANZ saw no reproductive effects in a dedicated screening study; EFSA judged the 90-day testis signal unresolved. Neither regulator found evidence of harm in humans.
- **EU/UK since 2024:** the UK FSA (June 2024) and Ireland's FSAI (October 2024) ruled that simple *water decoctions* of monk fruit are "not novel" (eaten in Europe before 1997), so they can be sold without new safety data; a 2025 review reports one specific aqueous extract authorised under Regulation (EU) 2024/2345, while highly purified mogroside extracts remain unapproved for lack of the data EFSA asked for ([FSA determination](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/non-selective-aqueous-decoctions-of-monk-fruit-consultation-request-to-determine-novel-food-status-pursuant-to-article-42-of-retained-regulation-eu-20/non-selective-aqueous-decoctions-of-monk-fruit-determination-of-the-status-of-a-novel-food-pursuant-to-article-42-of-assimilated-regulation-eu-2); [FSAI decision coverage](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/irelands-food-safety-watchdog-greenlights-monk-fruit-decoction-sales-for-use-in-fb-formulations.html); [2025 EU regulatory review, PubMed 40870722](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40870722/)). I could not open the regulation itself to confirm the 2024/2345 citation; treat it as the review's claim. EFSA's FAF work plan for 2026-27 lists a new monk fruit extract application ([EFSA FAF work plan](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-07/Annex%20IV_12.2%20FAF%20Panel%20workplan%202026-2027_0.pdf)) - the testis question may get an answer then.
- No JECFA (FAO/WHO) evaluation and no IARC classification exist. The WHO 2023 guideline against using non-sugar sweeteners for weight control applies to monk fruit as a member of the class (see the stevia topic for that discussion); there is no monk-fruit-specific signal behind it.

**Animal toxicology**
- 28-day rat feeding study of a Luo Han Guo concentrate (PureLo) at up to 100,000 ppm of the diet (10%): no significant adverse effects; lower weight gain at the top dose tracked lower food intake ([Marone et al. 2008, via search record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/asx__28769291)).
- Reported NOAELs for mogroside V are mostly above 1 g/kg bw/day (summarised in the FDA/FSANZ dossiers). *Animal only.*

**Human trials (metabolic)**
- Tey et al., *Int J Obes* 2017: randomised crossover, 30 healthy men, drinks sweetened with aspartame, monk fruit, stevia or sucrose. The three zero-calorie drinks gave no glucose or insulin spike; over the whole day, total energy intake was similar because people ate more at lunch after the non-sugar drinks, compensating for the missing sugar calories ([PracticeUpdate summary](https://www.practiceupdate.com/content/effects-of-sweeteners-on-glucose-insulin-and-energy-intake/48522); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20161220/no-difference-in-glucose-insulin-responses-with-artificial-natural-nonnutritive-sweeteners)). A companion study on 24-h glucose profiles found the same neutral picture ([A*STAR record](https://oar.a-star.edu.sg/communities-collections/articles/16013)).
- Kaim & Labus, *Nutrients* 2025, PRISMA systematic review: only **five** RCTs (2015-2025) qualified; monk fruit extract lowered post-meal glucose by 10-18% and insulin by 12-22% *relative to sugar*, with no serious adverse effects; trials were small and short ([Nutrients 17:1433](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/9/1433); [PMC12073669](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12073669/)). These are "swap sugar for a non-sugar sweetener" effects, not evidence that monk fruit itself lowers glucose.
- No prospective cohort has examined monk fruit specifically (it is too new in Western diets, and cohorts like NutriNet-Santé reported only aspartame, ace-K and sucralose at molecule level).

**Gut microbiome.** In vitro, mogroside V shifted fecal cultures toward Bacteroidetes and away from Firmicutes/Proteobacteria, and a modified-mogroside sweetener raised Bifidobacterium and short-chain fatty acids - *lab cultures only, no human microbiome trial found* ([Nutraceuticals World on 2022 study](https://www.nutraceuticalsworld.com/contents/view_breaking-news/2022-08-09/monk-fruit-sweetener-may-have-prebiotic-activity-study-finds/)). Neither a harm nor a benefit can be claimed.

**The erythritol confusion.** Most "monk fruit" tabletop sweeteners are 99% erythritol or dextrose by weight, with a few mg of mogrosides. News stories linking "monk fruit sweeteners" to clotting and heart attacks are about the erythritol (Witkowski/Hazen, *Nat Med* 2023), not about mogrosides ([coverage noting erythritol is the bulk of monk fruit products](https://www.phillyvoice.com/erythritol-sweetener-heart-attack-stroke-blood-clots/)). That risk is handled by the erythritol recommendation of 2026-10-04.

## Dose in real products

- At ~250x sugar's sweetness, replacing a teaspoon of sugar (4 g) takes about 15-20 mg of mogroside V. A Monk Fruit In The Raw packet is 0.8 g of which almost all is erythritol or dextrose; the mogroside content is in the low tens of milligrams. Drinks (Bai, Vitaminwater Zero, Swoon) use it alongside stevia at similar mg levels per bottle (brands do not disclose exact amounts).
- A heavy user (70 kg) taking five packets plus two sweetened drinks a day would get very roughly 100-200 mg mogroside V = 1.5-3 mg/kg bw/day. Rat NOAELs are mostly above 1,000 mg/kg, a margin of several hundred to over a thousand. The EFSA testis concern arose at experimental doses far above this; its exposure worries came from an applicant proposing very broad use (P95 up to 71.5 mg/kg/day), not from current US-style use.
- Human trials used realistic doses (one sweetened drink or meal), so their neutral result does apply to real use - but only short-term.

## How the scorer treats it today

- **Not flagged.** No category in `scripts/flagged_ingredients.json` matches "monk fruit", "luo han guo" or "mogroside". SCORING_NOTES.md decision 3 grouped "stevia, monk fruit, allulose, erythritol" as "a philosophy call", unflagged.
- Not in `_SWEETENER_TERMS`, so `SWEETENER_PRIMARY_CAP` never fires on it - and no catalog product lists monk fruit first anyway (it is always a minor ingredient).
- "Monk fruit juice concentrate" / "monk fruit concentrate" (about 25 products) is not in the `added_sugar` term list, unlike apple/grape/pear juice concentrate. That is reasonable: monk fruit concentrate is used for its mogrosides and contributes negligible sugar at the amounts used (the declared added-sugar grams still feed `SUGAR_DOSE_CAPS` whatever the ingredient name).
- Effect: products sweetened only with monk fruit score as if unsweetened on ingredients. Monk Fruit In The Raw with erythritol scores **83.6** because of the unflagged erythritol, while the same brand's dextrose packets sit at **40** via the sweetener-first cap - the gap is about the bulking agent, not the monk fruit.

## Recommendation

**Keep as is (do not flag monk fruit).** The evidence does not support the artificial-sweetener severity 2: there is no human harm signal and no equivalent of sucralose's glucose-tolerance or sucralose-6-acetate findings. It should stay consistent with stevia (also unflagged, 2026-10-05); flagging one plant-derived high-intensity sweetener but not the other would be arbitrary. Two notes for the owner:

1. The EFSA testis/long-term-data gap is real but unresolved, not a finding of harm. If the owner adopts the optional class-wide severity-1 "non-sugar sweetener" flag discussed in the stevia write-up, monk fruit should be in it (terms: "monk fruit", "monkfruit", "luo han guo", "mogroside"); on its own it does not justify a flag. Worth re-checking if EFSA publishes an opinion on the 2026-27 application.
2. The anomaly visible in this catalog (Monk Fruit In The Raw 83.6, Splenda Naturals Monk Fruit 83.6) comes from erythritol, already covered by the proposed severity-1 "sugar alcohol" flag; whether tabletop blends should face `SWEETENER_PRIMARY_CAP` is a separate queued question.

Optional: include monk fruit in any "sweetened" banner (queued as its own item) so shoppers see the product is sweetened without a score penalty.
