# Store-bought dried pasta (refined semolina, whole wheat, enriched, fresh)

*Researched 2026-10-03 by the scheduled research agent. Web searches returned abstracts and summaries; I could not open every full text, so figures come from those summaries (and the 2018 meta-analysis numbers from its published abstract as relayed by search). Items I could not trace to a primary source are flagged.*

## Verdict

Plain dried durum-semolina pasta is not "awful for you". It is a refined grain, but a low-to-moderate glycemic one (GI roughly 40-55), and the human trial and cohort evidence does not link ordinary pasta intake to weight gain, diabetes or heart disease. Whole-wheat pasta is genuinely better on the cohort evidence for whole grains in general, and the scorer already rewards that (whole grain sub-score 100 vs 20). The real health variable is portion size and what goes on the pasta, not the semolina. Recommendation: **keep as is**, with one optional tweak (the whole-grain weight is fair; do not cut refined pasta further).

## Evidence grade

**Moderate** that ordinary pasta intake is roughly neutral (RCT meta-analysis plus Italian cohorts, but the RCTs are of pasta *inside low-GI diets*, not pasta vs nothing). **Moderate-to-Strong** that whole grains beat refined grains (large cohort meta-analyses, consistent direction). **Weak** for the processing and residue concerns (drying temperature, glyphosate) at real intakes. **Weak/preliminary** for cook-cool-reheat resistant starch benefits (small crossover studies).

## What the evidence says

**Pasta and body weight (RCTs).** Chiavaroli et al., BMJ Open 2018, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 randomised trial comparisons (2,448 adults): pasta eaten as part of low-GI diets *reduced* body weight by 0.63 kg (95% CI -0.84 to -0.42) and BMI by 0.26 kg/m2 versus higher-GI diets, with no weight gain from pasta ([BMJ Open via Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effect-of-pasta-in-the-context-of-low-glycaemic-on-Chiavaroli-Kendall/eb2e9d3caf37a8b8e7a736c47eef63773e493422); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/04/180403085034.htm)). Caveats: the effect is small, trials were of median ~12 weeks, and the authors say it applies to pasta replacing higher-GI carbohydrate in a low-GI pattern. Funded in part by pasta-industry sources (conflict to note; the authors disclose it, per the summaries I saw).

**Pasta and body size (cohorts).** Moli-sani and INHES (Italy, ~14,400 and ~8,900 adults; Pounis et al., Nutr Diabetes 2016): higher pasta intake was associated with *lower* BMI, waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio and lower odds of overweight/obesity ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nutd201620); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4973136/)). Cross-sectional, Italian eating context (pasta as a modest first course, olive oil and vegetables), so not proof of causation and not necessarily transferable to American portions.

**Refined vs whole grains (diabetes, heart disease, mortality).**
- Whole grains: each +50 g/day associated with about 23% lower type 2 diabetes risk; consistent inverse associations with CVD and all-cause mortality ([burden-of-proof study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12937-024-00957-x); [AJCN 2022 dose-response meta-analysis](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)10518-6/fulltext); [Circulation 2016](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circulationaha.115.021101)).
- Refined grains: meta-analyses of prospective cohorts mostly find *no significant association* with CVD, stroke, cancer, all-cause mortality or type 2 diabetes ([refined grain and CVD](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1050173822001116); [refined grain and T2D](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0025619622002634)). So the evidence says "whole is protective", not "refined is harmful". Note "refined grains" in these studies are dominated by white bread, rice, cakes and cereals, so pasta-specific estimates are thin.

**Glycemic response.** Durum semolina pasta has a compact protein-starch matrix that slows digestion; reported GI values cluster around 40-55, well below white bread (~70+) ([GI of pasta products overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8623826/); [commercial pasta GI comparison](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8465557/)). Overcooking raises GI; al dente is lower. Whole wheat pasta has more fibre but its GI is not dramatically lower than semolina; its benefit is mostly fibre, micronutrients and the whole-grain cohort association.

**Cook-cool-reheat.** Small crossover studies show cooling pasta (24 h at 4 C) raises resistant starch (one wheat pasta report: ~8 to ~12.9 g/100 g) and lowers the glycemic response, partly retained after reheating; a chickpea pasta study found GI 39 fresh vs 33 cooled-reheated ([chickpea pasta, PMC11596303](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11596303/); [crossover trial, PMC7022949](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7022949/); [type 1 diabetes trial](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/7/1152)). Small samples, short follow-up, a preparation tip rather than a product property, so nothing the scorer can use. (Some figures, e.g. "70% retained", come from aggregator summaries; I did not verify them against the papers.)

**Enrichment.** In the US, enriched macaroni must contain set amounts per pound of thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid and iron (21 CFR 139.115) ([FDA Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/media/94563/download)). It is a public-health measure (folic acid fortification), so it is a mild positive for nutrition, not an additive concern. Gluten-free pasta is usually *not* enriched ([Gluten Free Watchdog](https://www.glutenfreewatchdog.org/news/gluten-free-grain-foods-and-the-lack-of-b-vitamin-enrichment/)).

**Processing: drying temperature, bronze die.** Traditional low-temperature drying (29-40 C, 24-60 h) gives negligible Maillard reaction; high-temperature short-time drying (75-100 C, 2-3 h) gives measurably more furosine (an early Maillard marker, and slightly lower lysine availability) ([drying and Maillard in pasta](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248425251_Effects_of_drying_processing_on_the_Maillard_reaction_in_pasta); [mitigation study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0023643823005698)). This is a quality marker; I found no human outcome evidence that it affects health. Bronze-die vs Teflon-die affects surface roughness (sauce adhesion) and texture, not nutrition. *Mechanistic only, Weak.*

**Glyphosate.** Pasta samples in surveys have tended to contain glyphosate at tens to low hundreds of ppb, far below regulatory MRLs (10 mg/kg for cereals in the EU); EFSA monitoring finds under 0.3% of samples above MRL overall ([EFSA 2023 residue report](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2025.9398); [JAFC Canadian retail survey](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jafc.9b07819); [narrative review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666154325000146)). Some figures above come from advocacy-adjacent summaries in the search results (EWG), so I rely on the regulator data. Whether glyphosate is carcinogenic is contested (IARC 2A vs EFSA/EPA not), but the exposure from pasta is a tiny fraction of the acceptable daily intake. Organic pasta lowers residues; no outcome evidence that it matters here.

## Dose in real products

A standard serving is 2 oz dry (56 g, ~200 kcal, ~42 g carbohydrate, ~7 g protein, ~2-3 g fibre). Whole-wheat gives ~5-6 g fibre. Portion is the dominant factor: restaurant and home portions are commonly 2-3x the label serving. The trials above used typical-to-moderate portions inside lower-GI diets; the Italian cohort figures reflect smaller portions than typical US plates. This is the honest "not awful, but portion matters" message.

## How the scorer treats it today

- Category `pasta` has `whole_grain_matters: true` at weight 0.22 (`scripts/category_facts.json`). Semolina/durum-first scores whole_grain 20; whole-grain-first scores 100; refined-first with some whole grain further down 55; legume flours drop the sub-score (`score_whole_grain`, `scripts/scorer.py:975`).
- Enrichment vitamins no longer count toward simplicity (audit item A4, SCORING_NOTES.md).
- Nothing in flagged_ingredients.json targets pasta.
- Result: plain semolina pasta lands ~70-80 (Luigi Vitelli Penne 80.4, De Cecco Fettuccine 79.0, Barilla Classic Penne 67.9), whole wheat 95-100 (Barilla Whole Grain 100, De Cecco Whole Wheat 95.3), legume pasta ~100 (Barilla Red Lentil 100).

Is ~75-80 for plain semolina fair? Compared with the evidence, yes: it is "good, not best", sitting 20-25 points below whole wheat, which matches "neutral for refined, protective for whole". Cutting refined pasta much lower would imply harm the evidence does not show.

Minor oddities noticed (not evidence questions):
1. De Cecco "durum"/"hard" first ingredients score the same whole-grain 20 as semolina, correctly.
2. Legume pasta scores 100 because whole_grain drops out and simplicity/nutrition are perfect. Fine, but "100" for chickpea/lentil pasta vs 100 for whole wheat suggests no differentiation; the evidence on legume pasta is thinner (fibre/protein plus lower GI in short trials) so mild.
3. Barilla Protein+ (semolina first, legume flour, pea protein) scores 42-67, pulled down by simplicity and external_processing, not a pasta-evidence issue.

## Recommendation

**Keep as is.** The 0.22 whole-grain weight and 20 vs 100 split already express the one robust finding (whole > refined). Optional, owner's call: none of drying temperature, bronze-die, glyphosate or cooling is worth a score term. If the owner wants a stricter line, a modest rule would be a small bonus for >=5 g fibre/serving pasta, but the evidence base is whole grains generally, which the whole-grain score already covers.

## Products affected

Plain dried pasta categories: spaghetti/angel hair 50 products, mean 78.9; elbows/ditalini 23 products, mean 73.4. The low-scoring "enriched macaroni" products are mostly boxed pasta sides with sauce mixes (e.g. Knorr Pasta Sides 12-25), which are low because of sauce ingredients, not the pasta.
